Why the system changes your yield, not your tap count

Tap count comes from tree size alone. A tap hole is a wound, and the tree needs enough living wood to compartmentalize it. What your collection system changes is how much sap each tap gives. Buckets catch what drips. Gravity tubing catches more by keeping a sealed run to the tank. Vacuum, whether the natural vacuum a 3/16" line generates on a slope or a mechanical pump holding 20+ inches, pulls measurably more sap from the same hole, nearly doubling bucket yields in a good year.

The ranges this calculator uses come from published research: Cornell and Penn State for buckets, Cornell's Childs studies for tubing, and UVM Proctor's vacuum trials. The honest caveat from those same sources: the normal band is wide. Weather swings a season 25% either way, so treat the range as a planning envelope, not a promise.

Rotate your tap holes

Each year, drill at least 6 inches above or below and 4 inches to the side of old holes. Many producers simply work around the trunk in a spiral. A tree tapped thoughtfully will outlive the sugarmaker.

Sources: Cornell Maple Program; Penn State Extension; UVM Proctor Maple Research Center; USDA NASS.
