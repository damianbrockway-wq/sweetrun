Why 7.1°F above boiling?

Finished maple syrup is a sugar solution of 66–68.9°Brix, and a solution at that density boils 7.1°F hotter than pure water. That offset holds anywhere — what changes is the boiling point of water itself. It drops about 1.8°F for every 1,000 feet you climb, and it moves day to day with barometric pressure. A cold front rolling through can shift your draw-off point by a full degree while your pan is running.

That's why experienced sugarmakers don't memorize "219." They find water's boiling point at their sugarhouse — with this calculator, or by boiling a pot of water — and finish 7.1°F above it. Drawing too early gives thin syrup that can ferment; drawing late gives syrup that's over-density and can crystallize in the jar.

Verify with a hydrometer

Temperature gets you to the draw; a hydrometer or refractometer confirms density (66–68.9°Brix, legal minimum 66.0 in most jurisdictions). Use both — the thermometer to run the pan, the hydrometer to check the barrel.
